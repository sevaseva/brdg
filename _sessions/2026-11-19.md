# Session 2

Session two was about playing a contract as the declarer. The two other major
aspects of the game (bidding and defending against the contract) took the back
seat.

## Planning as the declarer

The first things the declarer **must** :) do, after the defenders make the
opening lead and partner's (**the dummy** aka the table) cards are laid out,
are:

- thank the partner
- count the **top tricks**
- determine whether the top tricks are sufficient for **making the contract**
- if we need more tricks, see where and how we can "develop" them
- **make a plan** for playing the entire deal
- *only then* is it finally time to play a card from the dummy to the first
  trick.

Planning is an important part of playing cards — both as the declarer and as the
defender. It is easier to make and follow a plan as the declarer, because you see
AND control both hands of your line. Defending is harder, which is why we tackle
the declarer's play first.

## Developing tricks

We reviewed a few of the combinations and maneuvers that let us "develop"
tricks:

- **Sequence of high cards**: if we have ♣KQJT in our hand and our partner has
  ♣5432 (four low cards), that's zero top tricks, but we can play clubs and
  collect three tricks after giving the opponents one trick for their ace.
- **Ruffing** or **trumping** cards with trumps
- **Finesse**: if we have ♠AQ and the opponent *before* us (the one who plays
  before we do) has ♠K2, we can "finesse that king" by leading spades from our
  partner's (for example the dummy's) hand and playing ♠Q if the opponent
  doesn't play the ♠K.

We played a few of the "Level 1 - Beginner" deals on
[Bridge Master on bridgebase.com](https://www.bridgebase.com/v3/anon/bridgemaster);
those can be reviewed, replayed, and analyzed again there: same deals and no
account necessary (with ads).

One of the deals we studied is set up below and can be played card by card.

## Sample deal

Dealer: **North**. The page draws the four hands and the auction below, and
**Play deal** lays the cards out in play order so the contract can be played
through one card at a time. You can hide the auction or any single hand.

A hidden hand plays for itself: it follows suit when it can, and otherwise plays
a card that is legal. It does **not** play intelligently — it will not count
tricks, keep a plan, or finesse on your behalf. Hidden hands are there to let you
play a lesson out quickly, not to defend or declare well.

```pbn
[Dealer "N"]
[Deal "N:Q4.K54.A654.KJ54 A65.QJT.987.9876 J32.A32.K32.AQ32 KT987.9876.QJT.T"]
[Declarer "S"]
[Contract "3NT"]
[Auction "N"]
1D Pass 2NT Pass
3NT Pass Pass Pass
```

## Reading the auction

- North opens **1♦** with 13 HCP and four cards in each minor. With 4–4 in the
  minors, the partnership agreement is to open **1♦**.
- South has 14 HCP, a balanced 4-3-3-3 hand, and no four-card major. South
  responds **2NT**, an invitational balanced bid.
- North accepts the invitation with **3NT**. North–South have **27 HCP**
  between them.
- South is **the declarer**, not North: South was the first player on the
  North–South side to bid no-trumps. North's 3NT only raised the denomination
  South had already named.
- North–South do have an **eight-card club fit** (KJ54 opposite AQ32), but both
  hands are balanced, so no-trumps is the better place to play. Majors > NT >
  minors.
