# Session 1

## 1. The table and the cards

- Four players sit around a table in two **partnerships**: **North–South** and
  **East–West**. Your partner sits **opposite** you.
- A standard 52-card deck, no jokers. Four **suits**: ♠ **spades**, ♥ **hearts**,
  ♦ **diamonds**, ♣ **clubs**. 13 cards in each suit.
- Cards rank high to low: **A K Q J 10 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2**. The **Ace is high**.
- Each player is dealt **13 cards**. Sort your hand into suits (alternating
  colors helps), high cards on the left.

## 2. High card points (HCP)

To see how strong a hand is, we count its honor cards:

| Card | Ace | King | Queen | Jack |
|------|-----|------|-------|------|
| Points | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |

There are **40 points** in the whole deck; an average hand has **10**. That
number is how we say how strong our hand is when we bid.

## 3. A trick

A **trick** is four cards — one from each player, going **clockwise**.

- Whoever **leads** (plays first) can play any card.
- Everyone else must **follow suit** (play the same suit) if they can.
- The trick goes to the **highest card of the suit led** — *unless* someone plays
  a **trump**, in which case the **highest trump** wins.
- Whoever wins the trick **leads to the next one**.

## 4. Trumps and "no-trumps"

- A deal is played either in a **trump suit** or in **no-trumps (NT)**.
- In a **trump** contract, that suit beats every other suit.
- In **no-trumps**, there's no trump suit; the highest card of the suit led
  always wins.

**The golden rules of following suit:**

1. You **must** follow suit if you can.
2. If you **can't** follow suit, you can play **any** card you like:
   - play a **trump** and try to win the trick (this is called **ruffing** or
     **trumping**), **or**
   - play a card of another suit and **discard** it (throw it away).
3. **You're never forced to trump.** If you're out of the suit led, discarding
   is always allowed.

**A warning:** failing to follow suit when you *could* is a **revoke**, and the
rules punish it automatically (we skip over exactly how),
whether you did it by accident or on purpose. You can *never* gain from a revoke;
in practice it nearly always costs you.

## 5. The auction (bidding)

Each deal has **two parts**: first the **auction**, then the **play**.

In the auction the two sides take turns, going clockwise, trying to name the
**final contract**. A bid names a **level** (1 to 7) and a **denomination** (a
suit or no-trumps).

- A contract promises **6 + level** tricks; in other words six, plus the number you bid. So
  **1♠ = 7 tricks** (1+6), **3NT = 9 tricks** (3+6).
- The denominations rank low to high: **♣ < ♦ < ♥ < ♠ < NT**. To outbid the last
  bid, go to a higher level, or name a higher denomination at the same level.
- The **dealer** speaks first. On your turn you can **bid**, **pass**,
  **double**, or **redouble** (we'll get to doubles and redoubles later).
- The auction ends after **three passes in a row**. The final bid becomes the
  contract. If **all four** players pass, the hand is **passed out** and the next
  dealer deals. That happens only 1–2% of the time in practice.
- The player who **first bid the suit (or NT) of the final contract** becomes the
  **declarer**. Their partner is the **dummy**.

## 6. The play

- The player to declarer's **left** makes the **opening lead** (plays the first
  card).
- Then (only then!) **dummy** lays their hand **face up** on the table, sorted
  into suits. Declarer plays **both** hands; dummy just plays the card declarer
  names.
- Tricks continue until all 13 have been played. Count how many the declaring
  side won and compare with the contract. (Sometimes the declarer or even one of
  the two **defenders** can **claim** all or a certain number of the remaining
  tricks, shortening the play — but that's tricky, so we won't worry about that
  for now.)

## 7. Balanced and unbalanced hands

- **Balanced**: no **void** (zero cards in a suit), no **singleton** (one card),
  and at most one **doubleton** (two cards). Three possible shapes: 4-3-3-3, 4-4-3-2,
  5-3-3-2.
- **Unbalanced**: everything else — it has a void or a singleton, or two or more
  doubletons. Examples: 5-4-3-1, 7-2-2-2.

## 8. Opening bids (SAYC, first taste)

To open the bidding you usually need about **12+ HCP**. The player who makes the
first bid is the **opener**; their partner is the **responder**.

These are the openings we'll use:

| Opening | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| 1♣ | 12–21 HCP, 3+ clubs (open 1♣ with 3–3 in the minors) |
| 1♦ | 12–21 HCP, 4+ diamonds (open 1♦ with 4–4 in the minors) |
| 1♥ / 1♠ | 12–21 HCP, **5+** cards in the suit |
| 1NT | **15–17** HCP, balanced hand |
| Pass | fewer than 12 HCP (nothing suitable to bid) |

**Where do we, as partners, want to end up?** Rough targets for the two hands combined:

- ~**25** HCP → game in a **major** (4♥/4♠) or **3NT**.
- ~**28–29** HCP → game in a **minor** (5♣/5♦).
- **Below** that → stop in a **partscore**.

(We'll cover scoring in a later session — for now, just remember: a **game**
carries a big bonus; a **partscore** doesn't.)

**What are our basic priorities when bidding?**

- Look for a **major-suit fit** (8+ cards in a major between our two hands). If
  we have one, we usually want to play in that major — even if our hands are
  balanced.
- If our hands are **unbalanced**, we usually prefer a trump contract of *some*
  kind — even a minor-suit fit is better than no-trumps, because we can use
  trumps to control our short suits (so opponents cannot collect many tricks in
  their long suits).
- If our hands are **balanced**, we usually prefer **no-trumps** — even with a
  minor-suit fit. (Looking for a minor fit is a low priority when the hands are
  balanced.)

In one line: **Majors > NT > minors**.

## 9. Forcing, non-forcing, and invitational

When you make a bid, sometimes partner *must* answer, sometimes they *may* pass,
and sometimes you're inviting them to decide. Here are the words we use:

- **Forcing (F)** — partner **must** bid again; passing isn't allowed. More
  specifically, "forcing for one round" — **F1** — means partner must bid once;
  after that, unless one of us makes another forcing bid, bidding can stop (but
  doesn't have to).
- **Game forcing (GF)** — we're committed to reaching **game**; neither partner
  may pass below game. The strong 2♣ opening (a future session) is game forcing
  from the get-go, but a GF situation more commonly arises in the middle of an
  auction.
- **Non-forcing (NF)** — partner **may** pass if their hand is right for that. If they pass,
  that's fine — the auction can end.
- **Invitational (INV)** — in the middle. Partner **passes** with a minimum, and
  **bids on** (not necessarily straight to game) with a maximum. An invitational
  raise — the jump raise, e.g. 1♥–3♥ — is called a **limit raise (LR)**.
- **To play** (also called a **sign-off**) — strongly asks partner to **pass**:
  "this is where I want to play." The firmest kind of non-forcing bid.

## 10. Some responses to openings

Over time we will learn A LOT more about the first responses and subsequent
bidding. Your SAYC booklet includes all of that, and full bidding-system
descriptions are a lot longer than that. For now we introduce the meanings of
*some* responses to the most common openings. How do we bid after the first
response? For now, we will wing it :)

When your partner opens, they're the **opener** and you're the **responder**.
With **0–5 HCP, pass** any opening. Otherwise, respond according to what partner
opened:

### To a 1♣ or 1♦ opening (a minor)

| You hold | Bid | Type |
|----------|-----|------|
| 6+ HCP + a 4-card major | **Bid your major** (cheapest first) | forcing |
| 6–10 HCP, no 4-card major | **1NT** | non-forcing |

(*Cheapest first* = bid the lower-ranking suit first: with both majors, 1♥
before 1♠.)

### To a 1♥ or 1♠ opening (a major)

| You hold | Bid | Type |
|----------|-----|------|
| 6–10 HCP + 3+ card support | **Raise to 2** of the major | non-forcing |
| 10–12 HCP + 3+ card support | **Raise to 3** of the major (a limit raise) | invitational |
| 6–10 HCP, no support | **1NT** | non-forcing |

### To a 1NT opening

| You hold | Bid | Type |
|----------|-----|------|
| 8–9 HCP | **2NT** | invitational |
| 10–15 HCP | **3NT** | to play |

(Later we will learn other responses to 1NT, such as Stayman and transfers.
For now, 2NT and 3NT are all we need.)

## 11. When the opponents also bid (competitive bidding)

So far our side has opened. But what if the *opponents* open? Or what if they
bid after we open? We don't have to pass — we can still bid, to fight for the
contract. That's called **competitive bidding**.

The goal is the same as always: find our own **fit**, and figure out whether we
want to **play** the deal or **defend** (let them play it).

One natural thing we can do after they open (we'll learn much more later) is an
**overcall**: bid our own suit at the lowest possible level. It shows a decent
5+ card suit and about 8–16 points (we don't need a full opening hand). Example:
they open 1♣, we bid 1♠ with 5+ cards in spades and 8–16 HCP.

And **pass** is always fine — often the best choice when we have nothing to say.

Today we'll mostly just *notice* this; we'll dig into competitive bidding in a
later session.

## 12. Laws (rules) vs. agreements

As you learn bridge, it helps to notice two very different kinds of "rules":

- **The Laws** are the actual **rules** of the game — the same for everyone,
  everywhere, and enforced. Following suit is a Law; break it and you're
  penalized (the revoke). So are "13 cards each," "play goes clockwise," and
  "three passes end the auction." You can't change these.

- **Agreements** (or **conventions**) are the meanings partners *choose* for
  their bids (during auction) and card play (during defense).
  SAYC is an example of a *set of conventions* (also called a
  **bidding system**). Failing to follow those isn't "illegal" and isn't
  punished by the rules, but it may — and often does — lead to bad results and a
  partner's frustration :) I don't recommend that you violate your pair's
  agreements intentionally. We will spend months learning not to do that by
  mistake or due to misunderstanding.

There is a notable **rule** that governs **agreements**: a pair's **agreements
can't be secret** from the opponents.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| trick | four cards, one from each player |
| trump | a card of the trump suit, which beats any card of another suit |
| to ruff | to trump a trick when you can't follow suit (same as "to trump") |
| to discard | to throw away a card of another suit when you can't follow |
| revoke | to fail to follow suit when you could — penalized |
| HCP | High Card Points (A=4 K=3 Q=2 J=1) |
| contract | the final bid — the level + denomination to be played |
| dealer | the player who deals and speaks first in the auction |
| opener | the player who makes the first (opening) bid |
| responder | the opener's partner |
| overcall | a suit bid made over the opponents' opening |
| convention | a partnership agreement about what a bid means |
| bidding system | a full set of conventions (e.g., SAYC) |
| declarer | the player who first bid the suit (or NT) of the final contract |
| dummy | declarer's partner, whose hand is laid face up |
| defenders | the two opponents of the declarer |
| opening lead | the first card played, by declarer's left-hand opponent |
| fit | 8+ cards in a suit between the two hands |
| void / singleton / doubleton | zero / one / two cards in a suit |
| balanced hand | no void, no singleton, at most one doubleton |
| unbalanced hand | has a void or singleton, or two or more doubletons |
| game | the bonus contract: 3NT, 4♥, 4♠, 5♣, or 5♦ |
| partscore | any contract below game |
| forcing (F) | partner must bid again — may not pass |
| forcing for one round (F1) | partner must bid once, then may stop |
| game forcing (GF) | committed to game — may not pass below it |
| non-forcing (NF) | partner may pass |
| invitational (INV) | partner passes with a minimum, bids on with a maximum |
| limit raise (LR) | an invitational raise (shows a limited point range) |
| raise | to bid partner's suit at a higher level |
| support | cards in partner's suit (e.g., 3-card support) |
| cheapest | lowest-ranking (cheapest first = 1♥ before 1♠) |
| to play (sign-off) | asks partner to pass; "this is where we play" |

## Homework! 🥳

1. Shuffle and deal a few hands and just **play them out** in no-trumps (or with
   a trump suit you choose) with whoever's around. Focus on following suit and
   winning tricks — not on doing it "well".
2. Deal hands and **count your HCP** until you can do it without mistakes — the
   four hands should add up to **40**.
3. Practice saying the **bidding ladder** out loud: 1♣ 1♦ 1♥ 1♠ 1NT, 2♣ 2♦ …
4. Bring one question to the next session — something that felt confusing today.
